## BEAT 1: THE DOUBLING (0:58 to 1:30)
[NARRATION] In November 2008, economists Steve Hanke and Alex Kwok estimated that prices in Zimbabwe were doubling about
every twenty-four point seven hours. (pause) Think about what that means. Your pay arrives on Friday. By Saturday, it
buys half as much. By the next Friday, it buys *less than one percent* of what it bought a week before. So how does
money melt like ice in the sun?
[VISUAL] A clock spins one day while a price bar doubles. A pay envelope shrinks day by day, Friday to Friday. An ice
cube melts on a hot step.
[BUILD] 3D
[ON SCREEN] Prices doubled about every 24.7 hours (Hanke and Kwok) ▪ Friday 100% ▪ Saturday 50% ▪ Next Friday: under 1%

## BEAT 2: WHY (1:30 to 1:58)
[NARRATION] Economists still debate every cause, but one is clear. The central bank printed money far faster than the
country produced goods. Picture an auction for one loaf of bread. If everyone suddenly gets more money, but there's
still only one loaf, the price climbs. Now imagine printing money every single day, while farms and factories produce
less and less.
[VISUAL] Stick-figure auction: one loaf, bidders waving bigger stacks. A printing press speeds up while the shelf of
goods empties. Where the comparison stops: a small "simplified" tag.
[BUILD] Stick figures + 3D printing press
[ON SCREEN] More money ▪ fewer goods ▪ higher prices

## BEAT 3: HOW PEOPLE COPED (1:58 to 2:40)

waiting even one day cost them. They swapped goods directly, for example a bag of maize for a pair of shoes. And many
started using United States dollars and South African rand instead. (pause) Real families lived through this, and our
respect goes to every one of them.
[VISUAL] Stick-figure market: a family hurries to a stall, a maize bag swapped for shoes, dollar and rand icons arrive.

## BEAT 5: MONEY CHEAPER THAN PAPER (3:02 to 3:40)
[NARRATION] Then a Johannesburg advertising agency did something the world had never seen. In March 2009, they printed
posters straight onto real Zimbabwean banknotes. Because printing on money was cheaper than buying paper. One poster
said it plainly: it's cheaper to print this on money than paper. That campaign won the top outdoor prize at the Cannes
Lions, one of the world's biggest advertising awards. A few weeks later, in April 2009, the Zimbabwe dollar was
abandoned, and people used the US dollar and the rand instead. But here's the part most people miss.
[VISUAL] A wall of notes becomes a poster (our own illustration of the idea, not the original artwork). A trophy glints.
The note fades to grey; dollar and rand signs step forward.

## BEAT 6: MONEY IS A PROMISE (3:40 to 4:02)
[NARRATION] Money is not paper. It's not metal. Money is a promise, plus the trust that the promise will be kept. Think
of a voucher from a shop that has closed down. The paper is still in your hand. (pause) But the promise behind it is
gone. When people stopped trusting the promise, the paper stopped being money.
[VISUAL] A voucher in a hand; behind it the shop's lights go off and the shutter comes down; the voucher's glow fades.
[BUILD] 3D
[ON SCREEN] Money = a promise + trust
